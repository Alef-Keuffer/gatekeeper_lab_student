The map: how Gene X crosses your gatekeeper

In a defense, the first question is almost always “walk me through it.” This lesson gives you that walk-through: four stages, one example and two separations. About ten minutes.

Feels dense? Do lesson 2 (foundations) first. It explains what the gatekeeper is for, what “contract” means, and which parts the professor gave you.

Valid is not true

The source sentence:

Gene X did not significantly increase the risk of Disease Y.

The model returned predicate=causes, negated=False and uncertainty=low. The JSON parses, every field is present and the predicate is in the vocabulary. It is still false.OWASP calls this LLM09 Misinformation: “false or misleading information that appears credible.”

Your Relationship contract accepts this candidate, and that is intended. One public test exists only to prove it:

def test_two_consistent_mistakes_pass_the_contract(self):
    # The point of the lab: this wrong candidate is structurally VALID.
    rel = make_rel(predicate=Predicate.CAUSES, negated=False)
tests/test_gatekeeper.py

The model made two mistakes: causes (the paper denies an effect) and negated=False (the sentence says “did not”). The two agree with each other: together they say “Gene X causes Disease Y”, a coherent claim that just isn’t what the paper says. A contract can catch a candidate whose fields break a rule that links them, such as negated=True with causes. It can never catch one that contradicts the source, because it never reads the source. A later stage has to do that. (Lesson 2 takes this apart slowly.)

Four stages

Contract → Checks → Report → Policy
  1. Contract (gatekeeper.py:54). A pydantic model. Malformed or inconsistent input raises ValidationError while the object is being built, so a bad candidate never exists as a Relationship. Lesson 2 opens it up.
  2. Checks (gatekeeper.py:96–255). Plain functions that take the candidate and return one Finding: a check_name, a status (PASSED, WARNING or FAILED) and an issue_code when something looks wrong. They describe what they saw. They don’t judge.
  3. Report (models.py:80). Just a list of findings, with one helper, has(code).
  4. Policy (gatekeeper.py:186). decide(rel, report, consequence) walks five rules in order. The first rule that matches returns a Decision: a disposition plus reason codes. It is the only function that produces a disposition.

The five rules, in the order decide() tries them:decide() first turns consequence into a required assurance (LOW→BASIC, MODERATE→STANDARD, HIGH→STRONG, CRITICAL→HUMAN_CONFIRMED) and branches on that. This lesson states the rules in consequence terms, which says the same thing.

#If…then
1report has INVALID_EVIDENCE_SPANQUARANTINE
2subject_id or object_id is missing, or the entity is ambiguousHUMAN_REVIEW at HIGH/CRITICAL, else PROVISIONAL
3report has a semantic mismatch (NEGATION_CUE_MISMATCH, EVIDENCE_CONTRADICTS_CLAIM, ASSOCIATION_AS_CAUSATION)PROVISIONAL at LOW, else HUMAN_REVIEW
4consequence CRITICAL and no human review done yetHUMAN_REVIEW
5otherwiseAUTHORITATIVE

You don’t have to memorise this table today; it’s on the reference sheet. Hold on to its shape: the rules are ordered, the first match wins, and the consequence picks the branch.A professor may ask why REJECT exists but your decide() never returns it. In your code, a candidate too broken to build is refused earlier, by the contract’s ValidationError.

Trace Gene X

This is the real output from running your code on the Gene X candidate. The quote is the whole sentence, the ids GENE:1 and DIS:2 are set, and the span is valid.

Rules 1 and 2 don’t match. Rule 3 does, so decide() returns PROVISIONAL at LOW consequence and HUMAN_REVIEW at MODERATE, HIGH and CRITICAL.

Now remove the checks. With an empty report, the same Gene X candidate comes out AUTHORITATIVE at LOW, MODERATE and HIGH. The contract let it through, and one regex is the only thing between a false fact and the database.

Two separations a professor will probe

Checks describe; decide() decides

This is design rule 1 in the README: validators return findings, and only decide() returns a disposition. Gene X shows why. The same NEGATION_CUE_MISMATCH means PROVISIONAL at LOW and HUMAN_REVIEW at HIGH. A check that returned a disposition would need to know the consequence, so every check would carry its own copy of the policy. With the split, the policy lives in one function, and you change it in one place.

Consequence, not confidence

This is design rule 3. The model said uncertainty=low about Gene X, and it was wrong. Its confidence is a claim from the same untrusted source.OWASP LLM05: “Treat the model as any other user, adopting a zero-trust approach.” So uncertainty_finding records it (a warning when HIGH or UNKNOWN), but none of the five rules reads it, and test_model_confidence_is_not_authority pins exactly that. What does change the outcome is the consequence: how bad it would be if this record were wrong.

Practice

Answer from memory: scrolling up first turns retrieval into reading. Your first click counts.

1. Gene X arrives with predicate=causes and negated=False. Why does the Relationship contract accept it?

  1. Its validator rejects only negated asserted predicates.
  2. Pydantic skips validators when uncertainty is low.
  3. The evidence span is checked after storage.
  4. Extra fields are allowed on this model.

_negation (gatekeeper.py:71) raises only when negated is True and the predicate is in ASSERTED. False plus causes is self-consistent, so the candidate passes. The contract never reads the source text; that is the checks’ job. (D is backwards: the model sets extra="forbid".)

2. What does every check function (span_finding, negation_cue_finding, …) return?

  1. A Decision
  2. A Disposition
  3. A Report
  4. A Finding

Design rule 1: validators return findings, and only decide() returns a disposition. A check doesn’t know the consequence, so it can’t know what should happen to the record.

3. Gene X’s report holds one WARNING, NEGATION_CUE_MISMATCH. The consequence is HIGH. What does decide() return?

  1. PROVISIONAL
  2. QUARANTINE
  3. HUMAN_REVIEW
  4. AUTHORITATIVE

Rule 3: a semantic mismatch goes to HUMAN_REVIEW unless the consequence is LOW. Rules 1 and 2 didn’t match: the span is valid and both ids are set.

4. Same report, consequence LOW. What does decide() return?

  1. AUTHORITATIVE
  2. PROVISIONAL
  3. HUMAN_REVIEW
  4. QUARANTINE

Rule 3’s other branch. At LOW consequence the record is kept, but marked PROVISIONAL. The finding is the same as in question 3 and the disposition is different: the consequence chose it.

5. Same report, consequence LOW, but this time the model said uncertainty=high. Does the disposition change?

  1. Yes, it moves up to human review.
  2. Yes, it gets quarantined as unreliable output.
  3. No, no rule acts on that signal.
  4. No, uncertainty only matters for critical records.

uncertainty_finding adds a HIGH_CANDIDATE_UNCERTAINTY warning, and decide() copies it into reason_codes, but none of the five rules reads it. The record stays PROVISIONAL. test_model_confidence_is_not_authority pins this (design rule 3).

6. Delete negation_cue_finding from the pipeline and rerun Gene X at LOW consequence. What does it become?

  1. AUTHORITATIVE
  2. PROVISIONAL
  3. QUARANTINE
  4. HUMAN_REVIEW

The span is real, the ids are set and no other check fires, so rule 5 applies. A false “Gene X causes Disease Y” enters the database as trusted. Keep this in mind for lesson 4, which shows what that regex misses (“didn’t”, “nonsignificant”).

Say it out loud

Look away from the screen and explain, out loud and in under 60 seconds, what the gatekeeper is for, its four stages, and why Gene X does not become authoritative. Then open the checklist and count what you hit.

Checklist: five points
  1. Valid is not true: Gene X passes the contract because its two mistakes agree with each other.
  2. The contract rejects malformed or inconsistent candidates at construction (ValidationError).
  3. Checks return findings. They never decide, and never rewrite the candidate.
  4. decide() turns findings plus consequence into a disposition; the rules are ordered and the first match wins.
  5. The model’s own confidence is never an input to the decision. The consequence is.
